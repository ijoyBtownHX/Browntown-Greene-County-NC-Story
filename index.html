1712 & beyond

Before it had a name, it had ground.

This was Tuscarora homeland. The creek that still runs near here marked the line drawn after a war and the people who stayed kept this land alive long before anyone called it Browntown.

1762 → 1816

Free, before the nation was.

Land records here go back before the American Revolution. Families of mixed Native, African, and European roots were free here and they stayed free, on this exact ground, for generations.

1900

One page. One family. Two answers.

Did you know

In the 1900 census, the same census taker wrote down a mother as white and her own children, three lines below her name, as Black. Same day. Same pen. Same family.

1909

She built the school anyway.

Nine years later, it is believed the same woman signed her land over for a schoolhouse years before any outside program came to help. She didn't wait for permission. She stepped in for the community.

Generations

They kept the doors open.

The Albritton, Mills, Tabron, Scott, Ray, Jones, Brown, and Grant families started and kept the stores that anchored Browntown, means of commerce and community, run by the people who lived here. We honor and recognize them.

1950 to Today

The platform is still here.

Between two old longleaf pines, there's a cement slab poured for dancing and gathering. People stood right here on weekend nights generations ago. It never left.

the platform between the two pines

So many of the families who carried this legacy still represents us in every way.

Isabel Brown, who carried stories for 103 years.
